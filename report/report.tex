Reactive wind turbine maintenance, triggered only after a fault is
formally logged, forgoes the opportunity for early intervention. This
work investigates whether a recurrent neural network trained on SCADA
sensor windows can detect an approaching fault early enough, and with
sufficient calibrated confidence, to support risk-based maintenance
prioritization across a multi-farm fleet, and whether such a model
generalizes to turbines and farms it was not trained on. We frame the
task as per-timestep risk regression using a Gated Recurrent Unit over a
farm-agnostic physical feature representation, evaluate it against
classical baselines and across multiple farms, and report an honest,
evidenced account of where the approach succeeds and where domain shift
between farms limits it.

GitHub repository: \url{https://github.com/thedarkstringg/team-Sigmoid}
(tag \texttt{v1.0-final}).

\section{Introduction}
Wind turbine downtime is costly, and maintenance triggered only after a
fault has been formally logged forgoes the opportunity for early,
lower-cost intervention. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition)
systems already record dense, high-frequency sensor data from operating
turbines, raising the question of whether that data alone is sufficient
to detect an approaching fault before it is logged, with enough lead time
and confidence to act on.
 
This work investigates that question directly, with two specific research
aims. First, we ask whether a recurrent neural network trained on
SCADA sensor windows can produce a calibrated, per-timestep fault-risk
signal that gives a meaningful lead time ahead of a logged fault, when
evaluated against an appropriate classical baseline. Second, and
central to this paper, we ask whether such a model \emph{generalizes}:
does a model trained on one wind farm's turbines transfer to a different
farm's turbines, with different hardware, different sensor
instrumentation, and different fault-labeling procedures, without any
target-domain retraining?
 
We frame early fault detection as a per-timestep risk regression problem
over a 24-hour SCADA window, using a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) trained
on a farm-agnostic, physically meaningful feature representation
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) so that the same trained model can, in
principle, be evaluated on a farm it never saw during training. We
evaluate this model against classical baselines and, separately, in a
strict zero-shot cross-farm setting (Section~\ref{sec:results},
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), using the CARE-to-Compare multi-farm SCADA
dataset~\cite{gueck2024}, which itself provides a published benchmark for
comparison.
 
Rather than reporting only a favorable in-farm result, this paper places
equal emphasis on an honest account of where the approach's
generalization breaks down and why. We find that validation performance
within a single farm is substantially stronger than held-out test
performance, a pattern that persists across six independently tested
model configurations and is not resolved by further tuning; we then show,
through direct measurement, that this is consistent with real,
quantifiable distribution shift in the underlying physical features,
fault-label characteristics, and turbine hardware across farms
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). We further show that this limitation does
not appear to be specific to the deep learning approach, since classical
baselines exhibit the same instability. Finally, we connect the model's
risk output to a practical decision-support layer
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) that combines predicted risk with degradation
rate, access cost, and asset criticality into a single maintenance
priority score, so that the project's contribution extends beyond
detection accuracy alone to how a risk signal could actually inform
maintenance scheduling in practice.
 
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the feature representation, model
architecture, training procedure, and prioritization layer.
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} describes the dataset, splits, and
baseline models. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the quantitative
results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} presents the cross-farm
generalization study and the distribution-shift, label-shift, and
turbine-configuration analyses that explain it, along with the practical
implications and limitations of this work.
 

\section{Related Work}
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems are widely used
for wind turbine condition monitoring because they provide dense,
low-cost sensor data without requiring additional instrumentation.
Bach-Andersen et al.~\cite{bachandersen2018} review deep learning
applications to wind turbine condition monitoring and find that
unsupervised approaches, typically based on learning a normal-behavior
model and flagging deviations from it, remain more common in the
literature than supervised classification, despite the promising
performance supervised methods can achieve. They attribute this
imbalance to practical difficulties in obtaining labeled fault data:
faults are rare, and labeling is inconsistent across operators and
sites. Elshenawy et al.~\cite{elshenawy2024} provide a more recent
comparative study of data-driven fault-detection methods, underscoring
that method choice and reported performance vary considerably depending
on the specific dataset and evaluation protocol used, which motivates
evaluating any given approach against an explicit, fair baseline rather
than in isolation.
 
Several recent works specifically target the SCADA fault-detection
setting used in this paper: sequence models over raw sensor windows.
CNN-LSTM and attention-based hybrids have been proposed for this task
using single-farm SCADA data~\cite{cnnlstm2021}, showing that combining
convolutional feature extraction with recurrent temporal modeling can
detect early abnormal operation. Closer to the multi-farm setting
studied here, a recent 1D-CNN framework was developed using a newly
constructed dataset spanning three distinct wind farms, explicitly
targeting cross-source generalizability as a design goal rather than an
afterthought~\cite{cnn1d_multisource}. That work reports that a hybrid
architecture combining CNN, bidirectional LSTM, and an ensemble
classifier improves detection on multi-source data relative to simpler
baselines. This paper differs from that line of work in evaluation
protocol: rather than training jointly on data pooled across farms, we
train exclusively on one source farm and evaluate zero-shot transfer to
farms never seen during training, which more directly probes whether a
model's learned representation transfers rather than whether pooling
diverse training data improves in-distribution performance.
 
The cross-farm generalization problem addressed in this paper is also
related to prior work on turbine-to-turbine transfer under limited
target-domain data. Generative transfer learning approaches, using
CycleGAN-based domain mapping to make one turbine's SCADA data resemble
another's, have been shown to improve fault diagnosis on turbines with
scarce training data relative to training a normal-behavior model on the
scarce data directly~\cite{generativetransfer2025}. That approach,
however, still requires some target-domain data to fit the domain
mapping. This paper instead evaluates a strictly zero-shot setting, using
no target-domain data of any kind, which represents a harder and more
conservative test of generalization, at the cost of not being able to
exploit target-domain adaptation the way a domain-mapping approach can.
 
Finally, this work uses the CARE-to-Compare benchmark~\cite{gueck2024},
which provides labeled multi-farm SCADA data specifically curated to
support cross-farm comparison of fault-detection methods, including a
published reference lead time against which model-detected alarms can be
compared. We adopt this benchmark's farms and general evaluation
philosophy, while contributing an explicit, quantified analysis of
\emph{why} cross-farm transfer is difficult on this data --- distribution
shift, label-generation differences, and turbine-hardware
heterogeneity --- rather than reporting transfer performance alone.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task Framing}

We frame early fault detection as a per-timestep risk regression problem
rather than a single window-level classification. For an input SCADA
window of shape $(144, F)$ --- 24 hours of 10-minute-resolution readings
across $F$ features --- the model outputs a continuous risk score
$S(t) \in [0,1]$ at every timestep $t$, rather than a single label for the
whole window. This framing is required for the lead-time evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:results}): computing lead time as the interval between a
predicted alarm and the actual fault requires identifying the specific
timestep at which predicted risk crosses an alarm threshold $\tau$, which a
single window-level prediction cannot provide.

\subsection{Feature Representation}

Raw SCADA sensor channels differ completely in count and naming across
farms (54--238 average-statistic sensors, depending on farm), which makes
a model trained on one farm's raw sensors architecturally incompatible
with another farm's data. To support the cross-farm generalization study
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), we instead map each farm's raw sensors onto a
shared set of physically meaningful, farm-agnostic features, computed
identically regardless of which farm the data originates from. The
canonical representation used for all cross-farm comparisons consists of
ten features: wind speed, blade pitch angle, yaw misalignment, gearbox oil
temperature rise above ambient, gearbox bearing hotspot over oil
temperature, generator-to-rotor speed ratio, grid power factor, and three
grid electrical-imbalance/deviation terms (current imbalance, voltage
imbalance, frequency deviation). An eleventh feature, power residual
(actual power minus a wind-speed-conditioned expected power curve), was
evaluated as a separate ablation but excluded from the canonical
representation, since fitting its expected-power curve requires
target-domain data and would violate the zero-shot constraint of the
cross-farm study.

Each feature is standardized (zero mean, unit variance) using statistics
fit exclusively on the source farm's training split; the same frozen
scaler is applied unchanged to validation, test, and any external target
farm's data, so that no target-domain information influences the
input representation.

\subsection{Sequence Model}

We use a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) network rather than a Long Short-Term
Memory (LSTM) network or a Transformer. The dataset is modest in size
relative to typical deep learning corpora (on the order of $10^3$--$10^4$
training windows depending on farm); a GRU has fewer learned parameters
than an LSTM of equal hidden size, since it merges the forget and input
gates into a single update gate and omits the separate cell state,
reducing overfitting risk on limited data. Transformers, by contrast,
generally require substantially more training data than recurrent
architectures to outperform them, since they lack a built-in sequential
inductive bias; we judged this an unfavorable trade-off at this dataset
scale.

The final architecture is a 2-layer GRU with hidden size 32 and dropout
0.3 between layers, followed by a linear projection applied identically at
every timestep, mapping the hidden state to a single logit:
\begin{align}
h_t &= \mathrm{GRU}(x_t, h_{t-1}) \\
z_t &= W h_t + b \\
S(t) &= \sigma(z_t)
\end{align}
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function. The model returns raw logits
$z_t$ during training; $\sigma(z_t)$ is applied separately at inference and
evaluation time. Architecture depth (2 vs. 3 layers) and a bidirectional
variant were both evaluated as ablations (Section~\ref{sec:results}) and
did not improve validation performance sufficiently to justify their added
complexity or, in the bidirectional case, the loss of real-time causal
deployability.
For the zero-shot cross-farm experiment, the designated source-domain
checkpoint was \texttt{checkpoints/farm\_c\_lr5e4/best.pt}. This checkpoint
uses the same 10-feature physical representation, a 2-layer
unidirectional GRU with hidden size 32, and dropout 0.4. Its validation
ROC-AUC was approximately 0.645. This checkpoint was selected as the
best-validation 10-feature-only configuration for the cross-farm protocol;
the power-residual feature was excluded because its construction requires
target-domain calibration.
\subsection{Loss Function and Class Imbalance}

We use binary cross-entropy computed jointly with the sigmoid
(\texttt{BCEWithLogitsLoss}) rather than composing a separate sigmoid
layer with plain binary cross-entropy. The combined formulation avoids
computing $\log$ of values arbitrarily close to 0 or 1 directly, which is
numerically unstable in floating-point arithmetic and can produce vanishing
or exploding gradients as the model becomes confident.

Fault-approaching timesteps are rare relative to normal operation
(positive rate ranging from approximately 2\% to 6\% depending on farm and
split). We address this with a positive-class weight, computed as the
ratio of negative to positive timesteps within the training split only,
to avoid leaking split-level statistics into the loss function.

\subsection{Masking}

A small fraction of timesteps within an otherwise-accepted window are
gap-filled (via forward-fill, or back-fill at the start of a window) to
satisfy an internal coverage requirement. Gap-filled timesteps are marked
with a binary observation mask and excluded from the loss calculation by
multiplying the per-timestep loss by the mask before averaging:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \frac{\sum_t \mathcal{L}_t \cdot m_t}{\sum_t m_t}
\end{equation}
where $m_t \in \{0, 1\}$. The same mask is applied when computing
evaluation metrics, so reported results reflect performance on real
observations only. Certain physical features (e.g., the generator-to-rotor
speed ratio) can produce extreme values even when correctly marked
invalid; as a defensive stopgap, standardized inputs are additionally
clipped to $[-10, 10]$ before being passed to the model, since a single
extreme value elsewhere in a window can otherwise distort the recurrent
hidden state at other, valid timesteps in the same window.

\subsection{Compute Budget}

Training uses mixed-precision arithmetic and gradient accumulation to
control memory footprint, and a bounded 24-hour (144-timestep) input
window rather than a longer history, in line with the project's compute
constraints. In practice, the combination of a small dataset and a
compact 2-layer, 32-hidden-unit GRU resulted in peak GPU memory usage and
training time well within the allotted budget (on the order of seconds
per epoch; see the deployment note in Section~\ref{sec:results} for
measured latency and memory figures).

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection}

We evaluated six configurations, varying learning rate
($1\times10^{-4}$--$5\times10^{-4}$), dropout (0.3--0.5), feature count (10
vs.\ 11, i.e.\ with the power-residual ablation), and architecture
(unidirectional vs.\ bidirectional). The final configuration was selected
by lowest validation loss, without reference to test-set performance, to
avoid selecting a configuration that happens to fit the test split rather
than generalizing. All six configurations showed a consistent pattern:
strong validation performance (ROC-AUC 0.57--0.67 across configurations)
alongside markedly weaker held-out test performance (ROC-AUC 0.40--0.48),
a finding discussed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

All experiments use a fixed random seed applied to Python's, NumPy's, and
PyTorch's random number generators. Model and optimizer state are
checkpointed after every epoch, with the best-validation-loss checkpoint
retained separately from the most recent one, to tolerate the shared
compute environment's training window ending mid-run without losing
progress or requiring a full restart.
\subsection{Risk-Based Maintenance Prioritization}

Early fault detection alone does not determine which asset should be
inspected first. To convert model outputs into an operational maintenance
ranking, we implement a risk-based prioritization layer that combines four
factors: predicted fault risk, degradation rate, access burden, and asset
criticality. The resulting score is defined as

\begin{equation}
P_i =
w_1 R_i +
w_2 D_i -
w_3 A_i +
w_4 C_i ,
\label{eq:priority_score}
\end{equation}

where $P_i$ is the priority score for asset $i$, $R_i$ is the aggregated
fault-risk estimate, $D_i$ is the estimated degradation rate, $A_i$ is the
relative access-cost index, and $C_i$ is the asset criticality score.
The coefficients $w_1,\ldots,w_4$ control the relative importance of the
four factors. Unless otherwise specified, the implementation uses unit
weights, so that the score remains directly interpretable as the sum of the
four normalized decision factors.

The fault-risk component is obtained from the model's timestep-level
predictions using a trailing temporal aggregation over the configured
decision horizon. This reduces sensitivity to an isolated high prediction
while retaining the most recent risk information. The degradation component
is estimated from the temporal trend of the aggregated predictions, allowing
an asset with increasing predicted risk to receive a higher maintenance
priority even when its instantaneous risk is not yet the largest in the
fleet.

Access cost is represented as a relative access-burden index rather than a
monetary estimate. The implementation distinguishes basic access burden,
transport and logistics components, and an illustrative offshore premium.
The default offshore multiplier is $1.5$; this value is explicitly treated
as a project-level illustrative parameter rather than a measured
industry-wide cost ratio. Higher access burden reduces the resulting
priority score through the negative access-cost term in
Equation~\ref{eq:priority_score}.

Criticality represents the operational importance of the asset and allows
the prioritization layer to distinguish between otherwise similar risk
profiles. Thus, two turbines with comparable predicted fault risk can receive
different maintenance priorities when their degradation trajectories,
access requirements, or operational importance differ.

The prioritization implementation is exposed both as a reusable Python API
and as a command-line interface. The API accepts the four decision factors
and optional weights, while the command-line interface provides the same
calculation from explicit numeric inputs. Unit and integration tests verify
the scoring formula, custom weights, deterministic behavior, vectorized
inputs, ranking behavior, and the expected monotonic relationships: higher
fault risk, degradation rate, or criticality increases priority, whereas
higher access burden decreases it. The prioritization layer is therefore
implemented as a decision-support component rather than as an additional
learned model.
\subsection{Prioritization Implementation and Verification}

The prioritization layer is implemented as a modular component in
\texttt{src/prioritization/}. The implementation separates prediction
aggregation, degradation-rate estimation, access-cost calculation,
criticality scoring, and final priority computation. This separation allows
each decision factor to be tested independently and also permits the complete
pipeline to be evaluated from raw timestep-level predictions.

The implementation provides both a programmatic interface and a standalone
command-line entry point. The command-line interface accepts fault risk,
degradation rate, access cost, and criticality as explicit inputs, with
optional overrides for the four weights. The default configuration uses
$w_1=w_2=w_3=w_4=1$. The resulting score is deterministic for fixed inputs
and weights.

Verification includes unit tests for the individual prioritization
components and integration tests covering the complete prediction-to-score
pipeline. The tests verify numerical scoring, custom weighting, invalid-input
handling, deterministic behavior, ranking consistency, temporal aggregation,
and the expected monotonic effect of each decision factor. In particular,
increasing access burden lowers priority while increasing fault risk,
degradation rate, or criticality raises it. This verification provides a
software-level check that the implemented prioritization behavior matches
the mathematical formulation in Equation~\ref{eq:priority_score}.
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}
\subsection{Dataset}
 
We use the CARE-to-Compare dataset~\cite{gueck2024}, a multi-farm SCADA
dataset published by Fraunhofer IWES and distributed via Zenodo under a
CC BY-SA 4.0 licence (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.10958775). The dataset provides
10-minute-resolution SCADA readings across 36 wind turbines spanning
three farms: Farm A (onshore, Portugal, 5 turbines, 86 raw sensor
channels), Farm B (offshore, Germany, 9 turbines, 257 raw sensor
channels), and Farm C (offshore, Germany, 22 turbines, 957 raw sensor
channels). Across the three farms, 95 total labelled event datasets are
provided (44 anomaly events, 51 normal-operation controls). Farm A's
fault labels derive from an operator-curated fault logbook with explicit
onset timestamps; Farms B and C's labels derive from operator-provided
operating-mode codes and service reports, with onset inferred from
mode-code transitions.
 
Given Farm C's substantially larger turbine count relative to Farm A and
B (22 vs.\ 5 and 9, respectively), Farm C was selected as the primary
training (source) domain for this project, with Farms A and B reserved
as external, never-trained-on target domains for the cross-farm
generalization study (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
 
\subsection{Data Splits}
 
Train, validation, and test splits are asset-disjoint: every turbine is
assigned wholly to one split, so no turbine's data appears in more than
one split. This is a stricter requirement than a random row-level split,
since it prevents a model from partially succeeding on validation or test
data by learning turbine-specific characteristics rather than
generalizable fault signatures. The split assignment is fixed once, before any model training, and reused identically across the baseline models
and the GRU, so that all reported comparisons use the same held-out data.
 
\subsection{Baseline Models}
 
Per the project's cross-track requirement for a fair baseline, we train
two classical models on Farm C: Logistic Regression and
HistGradientBoosting. Both are trained on the same underlying 24-hour
windows used by the GRU, aggregated into a fixed-length feature vector
per window (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, last value, and
change, computed per feature over the window), since neither classical
model natively supports sequential input. Class imbalance --- fault
timesteps are rare relative to normal operation --- is addressed via
balanced class and sample weighting during training, mirroring the
positive-class weighting approach used for the GRU
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), so that both the baseline and the deep
learning model are trained under a comparable imbalance-handling
strategy.
 
\subsection{Data Quality Handling}
 
Both Farm B and Farm C are documented to encode some missing sensor
readings as literal zero values rather than as null/NaN; this is not
uniformly treated as missing, since zero is a physically valid reading
for several channels (e.g., power output during a stoppage). Raw
minimum/maximum/standard-deviation statistic channels for Farms B and C
are known to be substantially less reliable than average-statistic
channels for a subset of sensors; the physical feature representation
used throughout this project (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is built
exclusively from average-statistic channels for this reason. During
development, a unit-mismatch bug was found and corrected in one derived
feature's validity threshold (\texttt{grid\_power\_factor}), which had
been calibrated for real kVA units while the underlying Farm C data was
on a per-unit normalized scale; correcting this threshold increased the
feature's valid-timestep fraction from 4.9\% to 80.7\%.

\subsection{Domain-Specific Evaluation}
 
\subsubsection{Lead Time and False Alarm Rate}
 
Using the threshold that maximizes F1 on the test split ($\tau = 0.355$,
selected by the same validation-F1 method used for the classical
baselines), the model detects all 4 fault events in the Farm C test
split, with a mean lead time of 357.5 hours and a median of 395.3 hours
ahead of the logged fault --- substantially longer than the
CARE-to-Compare benchmark's reference lead time.
 
This result must be interpreted alongside the false-alarm rate at the
same threshold: the model alarms on 100\% of normal (non-fault) test
windows. A model that alarms continuously will trivially achieve a long
apparent lead time and perfect detection, since it never stops
signaling risk; the long lead time reported above is therefore an
artifact of near-constant alarming at this operating point, not evidence
of genuine early, selective fault discrimination. This is consistent
with the model's weak ROC-AUC on the same test split
(Section~\ref{sec:results}, Table~\ref{tab:gru_configs}) and should not
be read as a positive early-warning result in isolation.
 
\subsubsection{Cost-Sensitive Evaluation}
 
Using a 10:1 cost ratio (a missed fault costs ten times an unnecessary
dispatch, following the same ratio used for Kamal's AccessCost
component in Section~\ref{sec:method}), the cost-optimal threshold on
the test split is $\tau = 0.54$, at which the model produces zero false
positives but only 12 true positives out of 10,611 positive timesteps.
At this operating point, total cost is reduced by 62.2\% relative to an
always-alarm policy, but only 0.11\% relative to a reactive-only
policy (i.e., taking no model-driven action at all). Taken together with
the lead-time result above, the model's two candidate operating
points --- the F1-maximizing threshold (alarm almost always) and the
cost-optimal threshold (alarm almost never) --- both collapse toward a
degenerate policy rather than offering a genuinely useful middle ground,
reinforcing this paper's central finding that the model does not
discriminate reliably on held-out Farm C data.
 
\subsubsection{Calibration}
 
The model is poorly calibrated on the test split: Brier score 0.238 and
Expected Calibration Error (ECE) 0.447, against a best possible ECE of
0. Predicted probabilities are compressed into a narrow band
(populated only in the 0.3--0.6 range across the ten reliability bins)
and are substantially overconfident relative to observed outcome
frequency; for example, the 0.5--0.6 predicted-probability bin has a
mean predicted value of 0.511 against an observed positive rate of only
0.020. This directly affects the prioritization layer
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), whose fault-risk input assumes the model's
probability output is a meaningful confidence estimate: at this
calibration quality, the prioritization score's absolute magnitude
should not be trusted, though relative ranking across turbines may
still carry some signal.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results indicate that the main challenge in transferring the fault-detection
model across farms is not only the difference in fault prevalence, but also
substantial differences in the physical feature distributions presented to the
model. The cross-farm analysis therefore provides an important qualification
to the in-farm results: a model trained on one wind-turbine population cannot
be assumed to generalize to another farm without validating the compatibility
of both the sensor distributions and the fault-labeling process.

\subsection{Cross-Farm Generalization}

The cross-farm study evaluates direct transfer from Farm C to Farms A and B
in a zero-shot setting. Farm C is treated as the source domain, while Farms A
and B are unseen target domains. The designated source-domain checkpoint is
\texttt{checkpoints/farm\_c\_lr5e4/best.pt}, a 10-feature, 2-layer
unidirectional GRU with hidden size 32 and dropout 0.4. The checkpoint was
selected using the Farm C validation split, with an approximate validation
ROC-AUC of 0.645.

The cross-farm protocol deliberately uses only the ten canonical physical
features. The power-residual feature is excluded because constructing it
requires fitting an expected-power relationship using target-domain data,
which would violate the zero-shot evaluation constraint. The standardization
parameters are fitted exclusively on the Farm C training split and then
reused unchanged for Farm C validation/test data and for the external Farm A
and Farm B test data. No target-domain retraining, scaler fitting, or
threshold tuning is performed.

This setup measures domain generalization rather than adapted target-domain
performance. Consequently, differences between source and target
performance must be interpreted together with the feature-distribution,
labeling, and turbine-configuration analyses below. In particular, the
target farms do not simply represent independent samples from an identical
sensor population: several physically meaningful variables occupy
substantially different ranges after application of the frozen source
scaler.

\subsection{Distribution Shift}

To characterize input-domain shift, we compared the ten canonical physical
features using the Farm C training population as the source reference and the
test splits of Farms A and B as zero-shot target domains. Because the Farm C
scaler is frozen, we use a project-defined compression factor as the primary
model-input scale diagnostic. Since the source-standardized features have
unit variance by construction, the compression factor is equal to the
reciprocal of the target standard deviation after application of the frozen
source scaler. Values above one therefore indicate stronger compression of
the target feature relative to the source scale, whereas values below one
indicate a broader target spread. The statistic is intended to describe the
scale change actually presented to the model and is not treated as a complete
statistical test of distributional equality.

The strongest shifts occur in the gearbox-related variables. The
\texttt{gearbox\_bearing\_hotspot\_over\_oil\_C} feature has compression factors
of $33.73\times$ for Farm A and $66.31\times$ for Farm B. Similarly,
\texttt{gearbox\_oil\_rise\_C} has compression factors of $16.12\times$ and
$17.95\times$ for Farms A and B, respectively. These values indicate that the
target distributions occupy substantially narrower regions than the variance
observed in the Farm C reference population after the same scaler is applied.

Other features show substantial but more heterogeneous shifts. For example,
\texttt{grid\_frequency\_deviation\_Hz} has a compression factor of
$6.98\times$ in Farm A but only $1.51\times$ in Farm B, while
\texttt{generator\_rotor\_speed\_ratio} has compression factors of
$2.24\times$ and $4.86\times$, respectively. In contrast,
\texttt{yaw\_misalignment\_deg} has compression factors below one in both
target farms ($0.29\times$ for A and $0.48\times$ for B), demonstrating that
the direction and magnitude of distribution shift are feature-dependent rather
than uniformly larger in one target farm.

The compression factor should not be interpreted in isolation, because it
captures spread rather than location shift. For example, the
\texttt{yaw\_misalignment\_deg} feature has a compression factor below one,
yet its target means are substantially displaced from the Farm C reference
population. Likewise, the generator-to-rotor speed ratio exhibits both a
large change in mean and a substantial change in spread. These cases
illustrate why the analysis considers both scaled distributional summaries
and the compression factor rather than reducing domain shift to a single
scalar statistic.

The raw statistics further illustrate these differences. For example, the
mean generator-to-rotor speed ratio is $118.785$ in Farm C, compared with
$113.002$ in Farm A and $115.516$ in Farm B. Likewise, the mean gearbox oil
temperature rise is $44.689$ in Farm C, compared with $29.697$ in Farm A and
$42.271$ in Farm B. The particularly large compression factors for the two
gearbox-related features are also associated with unusually large source-domain
variance in Farm C, which suggests that data-quality characteristics of the
source population may contribute to the observed shift. Consequently, these
statistics should be interpreted as evidence of domain mismatch rather than as
evidence that the underlying physical relationships themselves are incorrect.

\subsection{Label Shift}

Input distribution shift is accompanied by differences in fault-label
prevalence across the three farms. On the respective test splits, Farm A has a
positive rate of $5.61\%$, Farm C has $3.65\%$, and Farm B has $2.08\%$.
Thus, the highest positive rate is approximately $2.70\times$ the lowest.
The corresponding numbers of fault events are 10 for Farm A, 4 for Farm C,
and 6 for Farm B.

The event durations also differ. The median fault-event duration is
3,120 timesteps for Farm A, 3,043 timesteps for Farm C, and 4,631.5 timesteps
for Farm B. These differences matter when interpreting per-timestep metrics,
because the number and duration of positive intervals affect the effective
class balance and the temporal structure of the prediction problem.

Importantly, the label-generation procedures are not identical across all
farms. Farm A uses an EDP fault logbook containing explicit onset timestamps
curated by the site operator from SCADA alarms and maintenance records. Farms B
and C instead use operator-provided operating-mode codes and service reports,
with fault onset inferred from mode-code transitions. Therefore, differences
in positive rates and event boundaries cannot automatically be interpreted as
differences in the underlying physical fault process. 
For this reason, we use the term ``label shift'' descriptively for the
observed differences in timestep-level label prevalence and event duration,
rather than claiming that the underlying fault-generating distribution has
been shown to change causally between farms. Part of the observed
label shift may arise from differences in how faults are recorded and
converted into timestep-level labels.

\subsection{Turbine and Sensor Configuration Heterogeneity}

The farms also differ substantially in their physical scale and sensor
configuration. Farm A is an onshore farm in Portugal with 5 turbines and
86 raw sensors. Farm B is an offshore farm in Germany with 9 turbines and
257 raw sensors, whereas Farm C is an offshore farm in Germany with 22
turbines and 957 raw sensors. The large differences in turbine and raw-sensor
counts indicate that the source and target datasets represent substantially
different monitoring configurations.

The generator-to-rotor speed ratio provides additional evidence of
farm-specific hardware characteristics. Its mean is $118.785$ in Farm C,
$113.002$ in Farm A, and $115.516$ in Farm B, while the corresponding standard
deviations are below one in all three farms. The tightly clustered but
distinct means are consistent with farm-specific gearbox ratios and support
the interpretation that the farms should not be treated as identical turbine
populations. This is also consistent with the sensor-mapping documentation,
which notes that nominal gear ratios may differ across datasets.

However, the available metadata does not establish a complete one-to-one
mapping of turbine manufacturer, model, and configuration across all farms.
Therefore, the analysis does not claim that every turbine model is uniquely
identified. Instead, the evidence supports the more conservative conclusion
that turbine populations and monitoring configurations differ materially
between the source and target domains.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

These findings have direct implications for deployment of SCADA-based early
fault detection. A model that performs well on a source farm may encounter
substantially different feature distributions, fault prevalences, event
definitions, and turbine configurations when deployed at a new site. In
particular, applying the source scaler without adaptation can produce target
features whose variance and mean differ markedly from those seen during
training.

Consequently, cross-farm deployment should include a domain-validation stage
before relying on model scores for maintenance decisions. At minimum, the
physical feature distributions and label-generation process should be audited
for the target farm. Where sufficient target-domain data are available,
calibration or controlled adaptation should be evaluated rather than assuming
that the source model is directly transferable.

This observation also motivates the risk-based prioritization layer developed
in this work. A fault-risk score should not be interpreted independently of
degradation rate, access burden, and asset criticality. The implemented
prioritization score combines these factors as

\begin{equation}
P_i =
w_1 R_i +
w_2 D_i -
w_3 A_i +
w_4 C_i ,
\end{equation}

where increasing predicted risk, degradation, or criticality raises priority,
while increasing access burden reduces it. This formulation is intentionally
a decision-support layer rather than a learned replacement for operational
judgement. In particular, the access-cost component is a relative index and
the default offshore premium is illustrative; it should be replaced by
site-specific operational cost information before deployment. Thus, the
prioritization layer demonstrates how model outputs can be connected to
maintenance decision logic without claiming that a single risk score is
sufficient for real-world scheduling.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations constrain the conclusions of this study. First, the
cross-farm evaluation is intentionally zero-shot and therefore does not test
whether a small amount of target-domain adaptation could recover performance.
The results should consequently be interpreted as a measure of direct
transferability rather than the best achievable performance on Farms A and B.

Second, the distribution-shift analysis uses compression factor as its primary
statistic. This choice is motivated by the frozen-scaler deployment setting:
the statistic measures changes in the feature spread actually presented to the
model and avoids allowing unusually large source-domain variance to mask a
target-domain shift. It should therefore not be interpreted as a complete
statistical test of distributional equality.

Third, several of the largest shifts involve gearbox-related variables for
which the Farm C source population has unusually large raw variance. This
raises the possibility that data-quality artifacts contribute to the apparent
domain mismatch. The analysis identifies this issue but does not establish its
cause.

Fourth, the farms differ in their label-generation procedures. Farm A uses
logbook-based fault onset information, whereas Farms B and C derive labels from
operating-mode codes and service reports. As a result, cross-farm differences
in measured fault prevalence and event duration may reflect both physical
failure processes and differences in annotation methodology.

Finally, the available turbine metadata is not sufficiently detailed to
establish complete manufacturer/model correspondence across all turbines.
The turbine-count, sensor-count, and gearbox-ratio evidence demonstrates
heterogeneity but does not by itself identify the causal source of the model's
cross-farm degradation. Larger multi-farm datasets with harmonized fault
definitions, richer turbine metadata, and prospective validation would be
needed to separate these effects and determine how much performance can be
recovered through calibration or domain adaptation.

The maintenance prioritization layer also has important scope limitations.
Its access-cost component is a relative burden index rather than a calibrated
monetary maintenance cost, and the default offshore premium is an
illustrative project parameter. Similarly, asset criticality is treated as
an externally supplied decision factor rather than learned from historical
maintenance outcomes. The prioritization results should therefore be viewed
as a transparent decision-support mechanism whose parameters require
site-specific calibration before operational use.

\section{Conclusion}
This work asked whether a recurrent neural network trained on multi-farm
SCADA data can detect an approaching wind turbine fault early enough to
support risk-based maintenance prioritization, and whether such a model
generalizes to turbines and farms it was not trained on. We framed the
task as per-timestep risk regression using a GRU over a ten-feature,
farm-agnostic physical representation, selected the final model by
validation performance across six tested configurations, and evaluated it
against classical baselines both within a single farm and, more
critically, in a strict zero-shot transfer setting to two unseen farms.
 
The honest headline finding is that generalization, not raw detection
accuracy, is the binding constraint on this approach. Validation
performance was consistently reasonable, but held-out test and cross-farm
performance were consistently weak, a pattern that persisted regardless
of architecture depth, learning rate, dropout, or feature-set changes,
and that was not unique to the GRU: classical baselines trained on the
same data showed the same instability. Rather than treating this as an
unexplained failure, we traced it to measurable causes: real,
farm-specific differences in physical sensor distributions (compression
factors as high as $66\times$ under a frozen source scaler), in
fault-label prevalence and derivation methodology (a $2.70\times$ spread
in positive rate across farms, with different labeling procedures between
farms), and in turbine hardware itself (distinct, tightly-clustered
gearbox ratios per farm). Taken together, these findings support a
specific, actionable conclusion: a fault-detection model trained on one
wind farm should not be assumed transferable to another without first
auditing the compatibility of sensor distributions, label definitions,
and turbine configuration between source and target sites.
 
This project also demonstrates that a model's fault-risk output is not,
by itself, a complete maintenance decision. The risk-based prioritization
layer developed in this work (Section~\ref{sec:method}) shows how
predicted risk can be combined with degradation trend, access cost, and
asset criticality into a single, interpretable priority score, providing
a decision-support mechanism rather than an additional black-box model.
 
Future work could evaluate whether a small amount of target-domain
calibration recovers cross-farm performance, whether the distribution
shift observed in gearbox-related features reflects genuine hardware
differences versus source-domain data-quality artifacts, and whether a
larger, harmonized multi-farm dataset with richer turbine metadata could
support a more direct test of what specifically limits transfer. More
broadly, this work suggests that reporting only in-domain performance for
SCADA-based fault detection risks overstating real-world deployability,
and that cross-domain evaluation should be treated as a standard, not
optional, part of validating such systems before deployment.
